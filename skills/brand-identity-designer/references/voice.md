# Voice, messaging, taglines and naming

Verbal identity is decided from the platform (`strategy.md`), not invented in a workshop. Evidence tags: `[practice]` studio doctrine, `[limited]` weak or small evidence, use as a prompt only.

## Voice versus tone
| | Voice | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| What | the brand's personality in words | how that personality adjusts to the moment |
| Changes? | no, same in an ad and an invoice | yes, with the reader's situation and emotion |
| Set by | the platform's ranked traits | the context: who reads, what just happened, what they do next |
- Decision rule when unsure: what is the reader feeling now, and what must they do next? Write that, in the brand's voice.
- A playful brand is still itself when a payment fails, but it does not joke about it.

## Calibrate the voice (write the numbers down)
Place the brand on each scale from 1 to 5. Numbers settle later arguments between writers.
| 1 | 5 | Example: law firm | Example: pet grooming van |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal | Casual | 2 | 4 |
| Serious | Playful | 1 | 4 |
| Reserved | Enthusiastic | 2 | 4 |
| Technical | Plain | 3 | 5 |
| Respectful of convention | Irreverent | 1 | 3 |
| Brief | Expansive | 2 | 2 |
- Record as `Formal 2 · Serious 1 · Reserved 2 · Technical 3 · Convention 1 · Brief 2`. A trait ranked #1 on the platform should show as an extreme (1 or 5) on at least one scale.
- Never place everything at 3: that is no voice.

## Find the voice in real language
1. Collect: customer reviews, sales call notes, the owner's emails, support replies, the founder explaining the business aloud. Reviews give the exact words for the problem and the result; those words go in headlines.
2. Have the client mark lines that sound like them and lines that make them wince. Patterns appear within 20 lines.
3. Map each ranked trait to a voice chart row.
4. Write the "we sound like" line as a specific person ("a senior nurse explaining a procedure: calm, exact, kind"), never an adjective pair ("professional and friendly").
5. Rewrite 3 real pieces (a hero line, an error message, a sales email), show before and after, adjust the chart from the pushback.

## Voice chart format
Line above: "We sound like [person]. We never sound like [bad model]." Table rows: trait as "this, not that" · what it means · do (2 to 3 behaviours) · don't (2 to 3) · a real line of copy. Below: 3 or more before and after rewrites with the rule each shows.

Filled example (residential electrician):
| Trait | What it means | Do | Don't | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plainspoken, not blunt | explains the fault in words a homeowner uses | name the problem and the fix; give the price range | hide behind trade jargon; lecture | "Your breaker trips because the circuit is overloaded. A dedicated kitchen line usually takes half a day." |
| Precise, not cold | times, fees, warranties are numbers | state arrival windows and fees | write "fast" or "affordable" with no figure | "Diagnostic visit: $89, credited to the repair if you go ahead." |
| Local, not small-time | knows the area's houses | mention housing stock and nearby towns | folksy filler; apologise for size | "Older homes here often still have 100-amp panels. Adding an EV charger? Start with a panel check." |
- Before: "We provide top-quality electrical solutions for all your residential needs." After: "Repairs, panel upgrades and EV chargers for homes across the county." Rule: name the services, cut the adjectives.

## Tone by context
| Context | Reader state | Tone | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homepage hero | scanning, deciding to stay | confident, specific, brief | slogans with no information, "welcome to" |
| Service and pricing pages | comparing | clear, direct, honest about price | adjective stacks, hidden fees |
| Forms and booking | task mode | neutral, exact | personality in labels, clever placeholders |
| Errors | frustrated | calm, plain, how to fix | blame, jokes, "Oops", codes alone |
| Success | relieved | warm, brief, what happens next | over-celebration |
| Support and complaints | upset | empathetic, accountable, a timeline | scripted apology, passive voice hiding who is responsible |
| Bad news (outage, price rise) | concerned | say it first, then impact, then options | burying it under a cheerful opening |
| Legal | checking terms | plain, plain-language summary on top | legalese where plain words suffice (confirm with counsel) |
| Social | casual | the most relaxed version of the voice | corporate announcements, chasing memes |
Microcopy strings inside a product belong to `ui-ux-visual-designer`.

## Headlines
Formulas (structure only; the specificity does the work):
| Formula | Fits | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome + audience | any hero | "Month-end close in three days, for teams without a controller" |
| What + where + promise | local services | "Same-day boiler repair in Leeds. Price agreed before we start." |
| Real number | proof is impressive | "1,140 pools built since 2004" (only if true and sourced) |
| Problem in the customer's words | urgent problems | "Gutters overflowing every time it rains?" |
| Before and after | transformations | "From bare lot to backyard you use every evening" |
| Anti-claim | categories with bad habits | "No call-out fee. No upsell at the door." |
| Plain instruction | clear first action | "Upload a floor plan. Get a quote in an hour." |
Tests, run all five: **swap** (competitor's name in: still works means not specific) · **so-what** (the answer is the real benefit, so use it) · **proof** (backed on the same page, else cut) · **read-aloud** (a person would say it to a customer) · **length** (hero headlines 4 to 12 words).
Retire: "Welcome to X", "your trusted partner", "[category] reimagined", "quality you can trust", "excellence in", "we're passionate about", "one-stop shop".

## Message hierarchy
| Layer | Length | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Value proposition | 1 sentence: outcome, audience, difference | hero, bios, ads |
| 3 pillars | 1 line each | must differ from each other ("quality, service, expertise" is one pillar) |
| Proof per pillar | 2 to 4 items | shown on the page: guarantee, process, credential, named result |
| Objection answers | 1 to 3 sentences each, 3 to 5 objections | in the customer's words from calls and reviews |
| Boilerplate | 50 and 100 words | press, directories, partner pages |
Never invent numbers; use proof that needs none when the client has no data.

## Taglines and slogans
A tagline is optional. Skip it when the name plus a descriptor already says it. [practice]
1. **Generate** 40 or more from: the onliness statement, the customer's exact words, the anti-claim, the promise kept, the audience's identity. Write 5 per angle, cut nothing yet.
2. **Criteria** (score 1 to 5, keep 8 or more of 15): specific to this brand (swap test) · true and provable · short (2 to 7 words) · sayable aloud, no tongue-twist.
3. **Tests:** swap test; "so what"; say it on the phone to a stranger, ask them to repeat it; check it reads at lockup size (it must fit under the mark at the minimum size, or it is dropped from small uses); translate it in each market language (below).
4. **Legal check:** search the exact phrase in quotes, then in trademark registers for the classes involved (USPTO, EUIPO, WIPO, the national register), checked as at the date of search. Common-phrase taglines are weak marks and often already taken. Lawyer before the client prints it.
5. Show 3 finalists, recommend one, never lock the tagline into the logo file; keep it a separate lockup.

## Naming process (advisory; a lawyer clears the final name)
Route choice:
| Route | Example shape | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | what it does + where | instantly clear, good for search | hard to trademark, hard to stretch |
| Evocative | a real word with a feeling | memorable, ownable | meaning may be crowded in the category |
| Invented | coined or altered word | trademark-friendly | needs budget to teach |
| Founder | person or family name | credible for services and craft | does not scale to a sale, spelling and pronunciation risk |
| Compound | two real words joined | easy to say, easy to spell | many are taken, can look generic |
1. **Brief:** audience, positioning, 3-year stretch (what it will sell later), markets and languages, the never list.
2. **Generate** 100 or more raw candidates across at least 3 routes (20 or more per route); no judging while generating; add a second pass from word maps, other languages and sounds.
3. **Screening funnel** (each stage cuts, record the reason):
   - Stage 1, gut and spoken (100 to 30): say it aloud, remember after one hearing, spell after hearing it, can stretch, credible for the category. Avoid fashionable suffixes (-ly, -ify, -io, -hub, -labs) without a reason.
   - Stage 2, linguistic (30 to 12): meaning, slang and sound in every language of the markets, checked with a native speaker (not only a translator); readable in the brand's scripts; no unfortunate abbreviation.
   - Stage 3, availability (12 to 5): `.com` or the national domain, social handles on the 2 to 3 platforms that matter, exact-name search results (the first page must not be dominated by another business).
   - Stage 4, trademark screen (5 to 3): register searches in the relevant classes and countries, reported as "no obvious conflicts found in [registers, classes, date]", never "cleared". Then a lawyer for the final pick.
4. Present 3 names with a one-line story each and the lead trait each serves; test with 5 people on hearing and spelling.
5. Avoid names that fail the mark: very long names (more than 15 characters) need a monogram or stacked lockup plan; tall ascender clusters and double letters need a kerning check.
6. Settle the name before logo work: a name change after drawing wastes the mark.
