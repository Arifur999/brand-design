# Strategy and discovery

Evidence grades: `[E]` published research · `[P]` practitioner doctrine · `[H]` popular or weak, use as a prompt only.

## Hard rules
- **No platform, no visuals.** Without a confirmed brief, draft a one-page platform from what you know, label every assumption, and show it with (or before) the first visuals. Otherwise strategy feedback comes back disguised as design feedback ("it doesn't feel like us").
- Even logo-only jobs get the one-page platform: it is what the logo is judged against.
- Trace every choice to a ranked trait or an audience need. If it cannot be traced, it is taste, and taste loses client arguments.
- Evidence beats knowledge-base defaults: when the brief contradicts a default, follow the evidence and log the override.
- Precedence when sources conflict: law and accessibility > the client's existing brand > sector conventions > taste (tie-break only). A client who wants to break the category changes the look, never the proof.
- Sequence with sign-off points: discovery (brief confirmed) → platform (approved) → routes (one chosen) → refinement (master files approved) → system and rollout. Small budgets merge steps; they never skip the platform.

## Intake
The questions that do the most work:
1. What do customers say about you when you are not in the room? (Read reviews for their exact words.)
2. Who is your best customer, and what made them choose you over the alternative?
3. Who do you lose to, and why?
4. What do you refuse to do that competitors do?
5. Where will people see this brand most? Rank the touchpoints (van, phone, shelf, deck, storefront, app icon, LinkedIn).
6. What must survive from the current brand, and what must go?
7. In three years, what will you sell that you do not now? (Stretch test for the name and mark.)
8. Who signs off, and will they see the presentation?
Also when relevant: three words it should feel like and three it must never; brands they dislike and why; real photos available or a shoot possible; proof inventory (reviews, licences, certifications, awards, press); regulated claims and required marks; languages and scripts; whether the brand shows numbers; budget and production limits (embroidery, vinyl, single-colour print).

Per component state: `new` · `refresh` · `keep` (a fixed constraint; build around it, report clashes as suggestions) · `none`.

Warning signs: "modern, clean, trustworthy" and nothing else (that is every brand: ask what it would never be); unknown competitors; a kept logo that fails one colour or 16 px (report it, do not change it). Not evidence: "our favourite colour", "everyone knows our blue" (a hypothesis to test), "red converts".

Gaps: decisive and someone can answer → ask (one message). Decisive and nobody can answer → most reasonable reading, written at the top, outputs marked "draft pending [fact]". Non-decisive → assume, log, continue. Conflicting inputs → ask; if nobody can answer, deliver both and flag; never merge silently. Keep an assumptions log: # · assumption · why reasonable · validate by · status.

## The one-page platform
If it does not fit on one page, it has not been decided.
- **Purpose:** could a direct competitor say the same sentence unchanged? Then it describes the category. Weak: "We provide quality plumbing." Strong: "We fix it on the first visit and tell you the price before we pick up a tool."
- **Audience by situation:** "[who] who [trigger] and want [outcome], but worry about [barrier]." Secondary audiences: what they need to see and where they meet the brand (referrers, recruits, investors).
- **Positioning:** For [audience] who [need], [Brand] is the [frame of reference] that [single point of difference] because [reason to believe]. Unlike [main alternative, including doing nothing or DIY], we [contrast]. One point of difference: three differences means none. The reason to believe is checkable (guarantee, credential, process, named result); "quality" and "passion" are not proof. The frame of reference is a choice that changes the whole identity ("accounting firm" versus "a finance department for companies too small to hire one").
- **Onliness test:** "We are the only [category] that [difference]." If it cannot be completed truthfully, narrow the audience or sharpen the difference. **Competitor-swap test:** put a competitor's name in; if it still fits, it is not distinctive. Without research, swap in the generic description ("a local HVAC company").
- **Personality:** 3 to 5 traits written "this, not that" with what each boundary prevents (warm not sugary: no exclamation marks or pastel overload; precise not cold; established not old-fashioned: no clip-art crests; premium not exclusive: prices not hidden; bold not loud; expert not academic; local not small-time). Build in **one tension** (precise + warm, premium + approachable); five traits pointing the same way is generic. Hygiene words (trustworthy, professional, reliable, quality) are not personality; prove them with evidence instead.
- **Rank the traits:** #1 drives type and colour, #2 drives shape and imagery, #3 mostly voice and motion; conflicts resolve by rank.
- **Voice line:** "We sound like [a specific person, e.g. the electrician your neighbour recommends], never like [a call-centre script]" plus one example line.
- Also record: proof we can show; category codes kept and broken; distinctive-asset bets (2 to 3 max); hardest touchpoints; mandatories; the never list; success tests as checks ("a stranger names the trade in 5 seconds", "the logo-off test passes on 5 assets"); open hypotheses.

## Positioning method by situation
| Situation | Method |
|---|---|
| Local service, personal brand, small market | Onliness + platform statement: fast, workshop-friendly |
| SaaS, agency, crowded or non-obvious offer with real customers | Dunford sequence: start from customers who love it; list real alternatives from their view (including spreadsheet, DIY, hiring someone); unique attributes with proof; attributes to value themes; who cares most; the market frame that makes the strength obvious. Do not position against competitors buyers never consider; prefer a familiar category to an invented label |
| Brand-new founder, no customers | Platform statement labelled hypothesis; validate after the first customers |
| Category challenge | Stated category / actual category (what customers compare at decision time) / opportunity category (a more ownable adjacent frame). Most advanced yet acceptable (Loewy's MAYA) |
Add a short "what this brand refuses to be" list.

## Distinctive assets (Ehrenberg-Bass) `[E]`
- A distinctive asset is a non-name element (colour, shape, device, character, type treatment, sound) that triggers the brand. It is scored on **fame** (share of category buyers who link it to the brand) and **uniqueness** (share of links that point to this brand, not competitors). Uniqueness matters most.
- Choose for ownability, not meaning: green for eco is what every competitor picks, and a shared asset is weak (Romaniuk). Category colour codes (blue HVAC, teal dental) are trust codes, not distinctive assets; distinctiveness has to come from shape, device, type treatment or an unusual pairing.
- Hold the form constant while recognition builds; vary only once the asset is known.
- Marketers overestimate their own assets' fame; group ratings beat individuals (rate independently first, then discuss). Prompted questions inflate fame; test unaided.
- Cheap reverse-recognition test for finalists or existing assets: show the element with the name removed, in a realistic context, mixed with 5 to 8 competitors' elements; ask "which brand does this make you think of?" with no list; recruit category buyers, not staff.
- The research is on large consumer brands; applying it to small service firms is inference. Positioning decides the message; distinctive assets carry recognition.

## Discovery exercises (with honest grades)
Review mining of the client and 3 to 5 competitors (exact phrases for pains, outcomes, praise) `[P]` · stakeholder interview with the final decision-maker `[P]` · competitor wall: 10 to 20 logos and home pages at the same size, in colour then grayscale; list shared colours, type genres, symbol subjects, photo styles; plot on two axes that matter to this audience; mark empty space and check it is empty because unclaimed, not because customers reject it `[P]` · personality sliders, write the numbers down `[H]` · card sorts `[H]` · Aaker personality dimensions as vocabulary only `[E, US 1997]` · archetypes as a prompt only `[H]`. Rule: break codes in the visual layer, keep them in the proof layer (a dentist may use terracotta instead of teal; may not drop the insurance list).

Turn attributes into 2 to 4 design axes (`routes.md`). Translate colour words into lightness and chroma first, hue last: established reads darker, fresh lighter; powerful is high chroma, gentle low chroma (Labrecque 2024 `[E]`).

## Trait to design starting points (combine the top 2 to 3 ranked traits)
| Trait | Type | Colour | Shape | Imagery | Motion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dependable | sturdy even-stroke sans, generous x-height, 500 to 600 | deep navy, forest or oxblood + one reserved action colour | small to medium radius, consistent stroke | real staff at work, eye level, daylight | low, no bounce |
| Warm | humanist sans or soft serif | warm neutrals + terracotta, ochre or coral | soft corners, organic | faces, candid, natural light | gentle fades |
| Precise | neutral grotesk with tabular figures, mono labels | cool neutrals + one saturated signal | small radius, hairlines, visible grid | close-ups, diagrams, real product | motion explains state |
| Bold | condensed or heavy display | high-contrast pair, black + one bright | hard edges or pills, diagonals | tight crops, movement | kinetic type |
| Playful | rounded geometric | 2 to 3 bright hues, each with a fixed role | large radius, blobs | commissioned illustration | slight overshoot on small elements |
| Premium | high-contrast serif display, light weights, tracked small caps | ivory, black, one deep colour; metallics as print finishes | no radius, thin rules, space | still life, controlled light, few large images | slow fades |
| Calm, caring | rounded humanist sans or quiet serif | desaturated sage, mist blue, sand; dark text at 4.5:1 | curves, nothing sharp | soft light, hands, unposed | opacity fades |
| Heritage | serif or slab with history | bottle green, oxblood or navy + cream | emblems, seals, symmetry | craft, materials, archive | minimal |
| Innovative | contemporary grotesk or variable + mono | neutral + one unexpected hue | modular geometry, controlled grid breaks | product in use | cause-and-effect transitions |
| Natural | humanist serif or sans with organic detail | earth tones sampled from real materials | medium radius, hand-drawn edges, grain | materials, outdoors, true colour | slow fades |
| Rebellious | unconventional display, tight leading | clashing pair or mono + one | hard cuts, stickers, collage | direct flash, raw | snappy cuts |
| Inclusive | highly legible wide-language sans (Atkinson Hyperlegible, Noto) | broad role-coded palette, all AA | friendly, nothing fussy | real community, with consent | low to medium |
Audience facts override personality: older or low-vision audiences need 18 px body and contrast well above minimum; urgent phone-first services need one reserved action colour and the phone number as a design element; multilingual brands need the scripts; regulated brands need reserved space for disclaimers in every template.

## Architecture, refresh and naming
- **Architecture:** monolithic (one name, default for small and mid businesses), endorsed (sub-brand + "by Parent"), house of brands (separate brands), hybrid (write the rules). Decide with five questions: same audience? does the parent's reputation help? could it be sold? budget to build a brand from zero? would sharing a name confuse? Co-brand lockups: divider or gap equal to the larger logo's clear space, matched optical weight, each logo follows its owner's rules and comes from official files.
- **Refresh or rebrand:** strategy changed → rebrand; looks dated but positioning right → refresh; fails small or in one colour → refresh; inconsistent touchpoints → governance, not design; trademark conflict → rename with legal advice; reputation damage → rebrand only if operations changed; leadership is tired of the logo → maintain (customers see it far less than the owner). Audit equity first: what customers actually recognise. A mark that is clip art, a stock template or a category cliché carries no ownable equity even if known: treat it as a new mark on a kept name. Roll out digital first, print as stock runs out, vehicles at the next re-wrap, signage last; update every local listing the same day.
- **Naming screens** (advisory): say it aloud, remember it after one hearing, spell it after hearing it, the founder can tell its story, it can become a strong identity, it can stretch, it is credible for the category. Before finalising: domain, social handles, meanings in the market's languages, a trademark screen, then a lawyer. Avoid fashionable suffixes (-ly, -ify, -io, -hub, -labs) without a reason.

## Creative brief (write before routes)
Platform summary · job the identity must do · ranked traits with the tension · competitor map findings (codes to keep and break, empty space) · distinctive-asset bets · primary touchpoint and hardest touchpoints · mandatories and never list · deliverables and depth · success tests · open hypotheses.
